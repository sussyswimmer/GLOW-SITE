The Mekong Delta lies in the south of Vietnam. Here, the Mekong River splits into many smaller branches before it flows into the sea. The land is flat and low, and it is crossed by a network of canals. These conditions make the delta one of the best places in the country to grow rice.

Rice needs warm weather and plenty of water, and the delta has both. Because the climate is warm all year, farmers can often grow two or three crops of rice on the same field in twelve months. The river also carries sediment, a fine material that settles on the land and keeps the soil fertile. For this reason, the region is often called the "rice bowl" of Vietnam, and it produces a large share of the country's rice.

Rice farming follows the seasons. In the rainy season, the river rises and water spreads across the fields. In the dry season, farmers rely on canals and pumps to bring water to the plants. After the rice is harvested, the grain must be dried before it can be stored.

Farming here also faces challenges. In the dry season, seawater can move up the rivers and into the fields. This is called saltwater intrusion, and too much salt can damage rice plants. Rising sea levels may make the problem worse. To adapt, some farmers are trying rice varieties that tolerate salt, changing their planting calendar, or turning to other crops or to shrimp farming.
